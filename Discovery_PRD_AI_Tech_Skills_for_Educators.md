# Discovery PRD: AI & Tech Skills Learning for Educators
### "Who is Training the Trainer?"

---

## 1. Problem Space

### Case Summary

This case examines how educators — teachers, trainers, and faculty — build foundational fluency in AI and technology, and why that process remains difficult despite AI adoption accelerating around them.

AI is no longer an optional skill for educators. Students are already using it faster than schools or teachers can respond to: in India, roughly 92% of students report using AI for coursework, while a large majority of teachers have received no formal training on it at all. India's own policy response — a nationwide AI curriculum mandate rolling out from the 2026–27 academic year across ~1.5 million schools — has made this urgent rather than optional, but it has also exposed a structural gap: the country needs to train over 10 million teachers, and doesn't have nearly enough qualified people to do the training.

What we are trying to understand is not "should educators learn AI" — that premise is already well established — but *why the learning journey itself is broken* for this specific persona. Early research points to this being less a content-availability problem (many free and paid tools already exist) and more a **structure, trust, and access problem**: educators report no formal guidance, tool overwhelm, competing time pressure against an already high-stress job, uneven access by school type and resourcing, and in some cases active skepticism toward AI itself.

The case is scoped around a broad "Educators" persona by design, anchored primarily in the Indian context, with the working hypothesis:

> *We believe educators need an easy and structured way to upskill with AI & Tech, to amplify the impact of teaching and keep pace with rapid AI adoption among students — helping build the right behaviours and encourage constructive scepticism.*

The immediate, measurable outcome this case pursues is educators building their own AI/Tech capability. The influence of that capability on student behaviour is treated as the longer-term strategic thesis this work builds toward, not something measured directly at this stage.

## 2. Stakeholder Mapping

### Stakeholder Table

| Stakeholder | Role | Why they're important |
|---|---|---|
| **Educator** | The primary persona — the one being asked to upskill | Their capability, confidence, and behaviour change is the entire outcome this case is designed around |
| **School Leadership / Administration** | Sets local policy, allocates PD time/budget, decides which tools are sanctioned | Controls whether an educator's learning attempt has institutional support or happens in isolation; increasingly uses "AI readiness" as a competitive/marketing signal to parents |
| **Government / Policy Bodies** (CBSE, NCERT, Ministry of Education, NISHTHA) | Mandates the AI curriculum, defines standards, runs official large-scale training (NISHTHA) | Creates the urgency and the deadline (2026–27 rollout) but lacks the delivery capacity to train 10M+ teachers directly — the mandate exists without a matching supply of trainers |
| **Students** | The downstream audience whose own AI fluency is outpacing teachers' | Their comfort with AI creates bottom-up pressure and, in some cases, erodes teacher authority/credibility when the gap is visible in the classroom |
| **Parents** | Indirect stakeholder — evaluate and choose schools partly on tech/AI readiness | Create institutional incentive (enrollment pressure) for schools to push AI adoption, while also raising data-privacy and appropriateness concerns that shape what schools will allow |
| **EdTech Tool Providers** (MagicSchool AI, Brisk, Diffit, Khanmigo, Coursera, etc.) | Build and sell the tools educators are expected to use | Represent the "current solutions" layer — but they assume baseline literacy rather than building it, which is precisely the gap this case is investigating |
| **Teacher Training Institutes** (B.Ed / pre-service colleges) | Should be the upstream source of AI/Tech literacy for new educators | Currently disconnected from the problem — AI-specific training isn't a required part of B.Ed curricula, so the gap keeps refilling with every new cohort of teachers entering the workforce |

### Current Ecosystem

**Current workflow — how an educator's upskilling attempt plays out today:**

1. **Trigger** — A mandate from school leadership or government (NISHTHA rollout), peer/student pressure, or personal curiosity prompts an educator to try to learn AI/Tech skills.
2. **Discovery** — They search in an unstructured way: Google, YouTube, WhatsApp teacher groups, a colleague's recommendation, or a one-off school-arranged workshop.
3. **Fragmented resource landscape** — What they find is either a single-tool tutorial (how to use *this one* AI tool), a generic video-based government module, or a paid course with no path — nothing sequences "what to learn first, then next."
4. **Attempt, largely alone** — They try to apply what they've learned without follow-up support, coaching, or a peer group working through the same material.
5. **Drop-off or shallow adoption** — Most either disengage (time pressure, overwhelm, low relevance to their subject/grade) or adopt one or two tools shallowly without building durable understanding or confidence.
6. **No feedback loop** — There's no mechanism to tell an educator whether they're now "AI ready," so progress is invisible even to the educator themselves.

**Stakeholder interactions:**

- School leadership and government both mandate training from the top down, but neither consistently follows through with sustained, role-relevant support after the initial push.
- EdTech vendors sell tools directly into schools/classrooms but don't teach foundational literacy — they assume it.
- Teacher training institutes remain structurally outside this loop entirely, so the gap isn't closing at the source.
- Students and parents apply pressure from two different directions — students by simply being more fluent already, parents by treating AI-readiness as a school selection criterion — but neither directly helps the educator build the skill; they just raise the stakes.

**Journey map (simplified):**

`Mandate/Curiosity → Unstructured Search → Fragmented Tool-Specific Tutorial → Solo Attempt → Overwhelm/Time Pressure → Drop-off or Shallow Use → No Confirmation of Readiness`

*(Note: render as a visual diagram in the final formatted doc.)*

### Insights

The current ecosystem has plenty of top-down pressure (government mandates, school policy, parent expectations) and bottom-up pressure (students already ahead), but no structured middle layer that actually helps the educator build the capability everyone is demanding of them. Tools exist in abundance, but they solve for task execution, not literacy-building — and the one institution positioned to fix this upstream, teacher training colleges, isn't part of the loop at all. The result is an educator stuck absorbing pressure from every direction with no structured, supported path to respond to it.

## 3. Secondary Research

### 3.1 Industry Trends

**Major trends:**
- **Explosive but uneven market growth.** The global AI-in-education market was worth roughly $5.5–5.9B in 2024 and is projected to reach ~$32.27B by 2030 (31–43% CAGR); India's EdTech market specifically is projected to grow from ~$7.5B to ~$30B by 2031. Growth is being driven by institutional/government spend, not primarily by individual educator purchases.
- **Policy is forcing the pace, not demand.** India's National Curriculum Framework mandates that AI and computational-thinking concepts be introduced from Class 3 starting the 2026–27 academic year, across all ~1.5 million schools, government and private alike. Similar top-down mandates are emerging elsewhere (China, UAE).
- **Teacher AI usage is surging faster than training capacity.** Teacher use of AI jumped from 32% in 2024 to 61% in 2025 in U.S. survey data, with professional development cited as a key driver of the increase. A 2026 survey of over 11,000 educators found 80% now report using AI tools in their classrooms.
- **Consolidation pressure on tools.** Educators report managing an average of eight different digital tools, and cite lack of integration between systems as their primary difficulty — the market is trending toward demand for fewer, better-integrated platforms rather than more point-solutions.
- **Credentialing is becoming a differentiator.** Verified AI/digital credentials are drawing a meaningfully higher hiring preference, pushing both individual educators and institutions toward certification-bearing formats, not just informal learning.

**What's driving the change:** regulatory mandates creating forced, dated demand; a widening visible gap between student and teacher AI fluency; school-level competitive pressure (parents evaluating "AI readiness" when choosing schools); and burnout economics — educators still lose an average of seven hours a week to manual tasks despite already having many digital tools, creating real appetite for anything that credibly saves time.

**Insights:** The industry is being pulled forward by policy and institutional pressure faster than the supporting infrastructure — training capacity, credentialing clarity, tool integration — can keep up. That mismatch (mandated urgency vs. inadequate delivery capacity) is itself the market opportunity.

The educator absorbs demand from all directions with no stakeholder accountable for supplying the capability being demanded.

### 3.3 Current Solutions

**How this is solved today:**
- **Government-led, mandatory:** NISHTHA delivers grade-specific, video-based AI training nationally; CBSE runs SOAR (Skilling for AI Readiness), an optional 15-hour AI module already active in over 18,000 CBSE schools for Classes 6–8. Strength: scale and legitimacy. Weakness: one-directional, video-based delivery with no personalization or follow-through.
- **Task-specific AI tools:** MagicSchool AI, Brisk Teaching, Edcafe AI, Diffit, Khanmigo, MATHia, SchoolAI. Strength: immediately useful, low-friction for a specific task. Weakness: assumes baseline comfort, teaches tool usage not underlying literacy, and contributes to the platform-fatigue problem (8 tools/educator on average).
- **General AI literacy courses:** Coursera/DeepLearning.AI ("AI for Everyone"), Microsoft Learn/Copilot Skilling Centre, AACSB Academy (for business faculty). Strength: structured, credentialed. Weakness: generic, not role- or classroom-specific; often paid; not habit-forming.
- **Open resource hubs:** UNESCO ICT Framework, LEVEL UP AI, Digital Education Council. Strength: free, well-intentioned. Weakness: passive libraries — still require the educator to self-navigate, which is the exact "where do I start" problem.
- **Informal workarounds (the actual dominant path today):** peer recommendations in WhatsApp teacher groups, YouTube tutorials, trial-and-error with whatever tool a colleague mentioned, occasional one-off school workshops. This is where most educators actually are — unstructured, unsequenced, and invisible to any institution trying to measure readiness.

**Insights:** Structured solutions exist at two extremes — mandatory government programs (broad but shallow) and paid professional courses (rigorous but generic and inaccessible to many) — with almost nothing in between that's structured, role-relevant, and low-friction. The gap is filled today by informal, unmeasurable peer learning.

### 3.4 Competitor Analysis

| Competitor | Problem They Solve | Target User | How | Gaps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **MagicSchool AI** | Task automation (lesson plans, rubrics, IEPs) | Any K-12 teacher | 80+ point-solution tools, no prompting skill required | Teaches tool usage, not literacy; 80-tool library itself becomes a navigation problem |
| **NISHTHA (Govt of India)** | National-scale mandated AI training | All Indian teachers (10M+) | Grade-specific, video-based modules | One-directional, no follow-up support, no measure of actual readiness gained |
| **Coursera / DeepLearning.AI** | Structured, credentialed AI fundamentals | Any professional, not education-specific | Self-paced accredited courses | Generic — not classroom- or subject-contextualized; paid; not habit-forming, no sustained engagement loop |
| **Khanmigo (Khan Academy)** | AI tutoring support + some teacher prep tools | Students primarily, teachers secondarily | Socratic AI tutor model | Built for student use first; teacher capability-building is a side feature, not the core product |
| **SOAR (CBSE, India)** | Structured, curriculum-aligned AI skilling | CBSE-affiliated schools, Classes 6–8 focus | 15-hour structured module | Scoped to specific grades/board; not designed as an ongoing habit-forming educator journey |
| **LEVEL UP AI / UNESCO OER hubs** | Resource-sharing for AI education best practice | Higher-ed institutions, educators broadly | Open repository model | Passive — still requires self-navigation; no sequencing, no accountability, no progress tracking |

**Insights:** No competitor combines all three of what the research points to as necessary: (1) structured sequencing so an educator knows what to learn next, (2) role/classroom relevance rather than generic AI literacy, and (3) a sustained, habit-forming cadence rather than a one-time course or workshop. Each existing player solves one dimension well and leaves the others unaddressed.

### 3.6 Observations

*(Patterns only, no interpretation.)*
- Teacher AI usage has roughly doubled year-over-year while formal guidance and training availability have grown far more slowly.
- Government mandates and school-level pressure are increasing faster than any institution's delivery capacity to meet them.
- Existing tools cluster heavily around task execution (lesson plans, grading, differentiation); almost none are designed around sequential capability-building.
- Training access correlates with school-level resourcing/poverty, not with teacher need or motivation.
- Every stakeholder except the educator is either demanding readiness or selling a task tool.
- Teacher training institutes (B.Ed / pre-service programs) appear nowhere in the current solution landscape.
- A meaningful minority of teachers (roughly a third+) remain unmotivated or actively skeptical, even as majority adoption rises.
- Multilingual/non-English support is largely absent across the tool landscape.

### 3.7 Insights

*(One level deeper — what, why, evidence.)*

- **What:** Adoption is outpacing preparedness almost everywhere we looked. **Why:** Because access to *tools* is frictionless (free tiers, browser extensions, no training needed to start clicking), while access to *structured learning* requires institutional investment that hasn't scaled at the same speed. **Evidence:** U.S. teacher usage jumping from 32% to 61% in a single year, alongside 70% still not feeling prepared, and 82% receiving no formal guidance.
- **What:** The "training the trainer" gap is a genuine supply-chain constraint, not just a motivation or awareness problem. **Why:** Because there simply aren't enough qualified AI professionals in India to deliver training to 10M+ teachers through traditional expert-led models. **Evidence:** India's ~650K qualified AI professionals against a need for 1.2M+ by 2027, set against a mandate to train the entire 10M-teacher workforce.
- **What:** Existing tools solve "do this task" but not "build this capability," so tool proliferation doesn't translate into confidence. **Why:** Because tool vendors are incentivized by feature adoption and retention, not by measurable learner progression — teaching someone to fish isn't their business model. **Evidence:** 8 tools/educator on average, 50% reporting overwhelm, yet 70% still report feeling unprepared despite that tool exposure.
- **What:** Access to structured training is an equity issue, not just an individual-choice issue. **Why:** Because school-level resourcing determines whether an educator gets institutional training time/budget at all. **Evidence:** 67% vs. 39% training provision split by district poverty level; a similarly wider readiness gap in Indian government vs. private schools.

### 3.8 Research Gaps

- We have no primary data directly from Indian educators on their own perceived skill level, specific pain points, or what "structured" would concretely mean to them — everything so far is secondary (surveys, reports, commentary), and much of the granular pain-point data (guidance, overwhelm, trust) is U.S.-sourced, not confirmed for the Indian context specifically.
- We don't know educators' willingness to pay, or whether the right economic model is individual, school-funded, or government-subsidized.
- We don't know the preferred channel for actual sustained engagement — WhatsApp, a dedicated app, in-person cohorts — only assumptions based on where informal learning currently happens (WhatsApp groups, YouTube).
- We're assuming the broad "Educators" persona holds together as a single wedge; we don't yet know whether needs converge enough across government/private, K-12/higher-ed, subject areas to design one first experience that works for all of them.
- We don't know what a credible "readiness" signal would need to look like to be trusted by schools, educators, or the market — self-assessment, testing, portfolio, something else.
- We don't yet know how educators currently feel about their own AI/Tech confidence in their own words — everything above is inferred from survey aggregates, not from a direct conversation with the target user.

These gaps point clearly toward needing a primary research pass (survey or interviews with Indian educators) before finalizing the MVP design.

## 4. Key Insights

*(Summary of the full Secondary Research section — insights only, no raw facts restated.)*

- **We observed** that teacher AI adoption is rising faster than institutional support in every market we looked at, **because** tool access is frictionless while structured training requires investment that hasn't scaled at the same pace, **which means** the real opportunity isn't "get educators to start using AI" — that's already happening — it's catching structured support up to adoption that's already underway.
- **We observed** that India's "train the trainer" gap is a hard supply-side constraint, **because** the country doesn't have nearly enough qualified AI professionals to deliver mandated training to 10 million teachers through traditional expert-led models, **which means** any solution that depends on scarce human experts to scale will structurally fail to reach the size the mandate demands — this needs a model that doesn't bottleneck on expert trainers.
- **One interesting thing we discovered** is that every stakeholder around the educator is either demanding readiness (government, school leadership, students, parents) or selling a task tool (EdTech vendors) — none of them is structurally accountable for building the underlying capability. This means the educator is currently expected to self-assemble their own learning path, with no institution actually designed to own that job.
- **We observed** that existing tools cluster around task execution rather than capability-building, **because** vendors are incentivized by feature adoption and retention, not by measurable learner progression, **which means** tool proliferation isn't translating into confidence — an educator can be using eight different tools and still report feeling unprepared.
- **We observed** that access to structured training tracks institutional wealth — district poverty level, government vs. private school — rather than teacher need or motivation, **which means** the current market is quietly widening the readiness gap it claims to be closing, since the educators who need support most are the least likely to receive it.
- **One interesting thing we discovered** is that a meaningful minority of teachers remain unmotivated or actively skeptical even as majority adoption climbs, **which means** a solution built for the broad "Educators" persona can't assume universal enthusiasm — it has to work for, or at minimum not alienate, the reluctant middle, not just the early adopters.

## 5. Opportunity Areas

**Opportunity 1 — Catch-up structure for already-adopted behaviour**
**For** educators who are already dabbling with AI tools but doing so without any sequence or plan
**Through** a lightweight, sequenced learning path that tells them what to learn first and what's next — rather than another standalone tool or a monolithic course
**Because** the friction to *start using* AI is already near-zero; the friction that remains is learning it systematically, and nothing currently fills that gap

**Opportunity 2 — A delivery model that doesn't bottleneck on scarce experts**
**For** the full breadth of the educator base (10M+ in India alone), most of whom expert-led training can never reach at that scale
**Through** a peer-led or AI-guided delivery format — using AI itself as the always-available "trainer," and/or structured peer cohorts — instead of depending on scarce human AI experts
**Because** India's training mandate already exceeds its supply of qualified trainers; any solution that still depends on expert-led delivery inherits the same structural ceiling

**Opportunity 3 — Own the "home base" for an educator's ongoing growth**
**For** educators who currently self-assemble learning from YouTube, WhatsApp groups, and one-off workshops with no continuity between them
**Through** a single ongoing product that tracks and owns progression over time, rather than being one more disconnected resource in the pile
**Because** no existing stakeholder — vendor, government, or school — is structurally accountable for an educator's capability building; someone owning that continuity is itself the differentiator

**Opportunity 4 — Build for confidence, not just tool exposure**
**For** educators already using multiple AI tools who still report feeling unprepared
**Through** a practice-feedback-validation loop (small wins, applied exercises, visible progress) rather than another point-solution tool
**Because** tool exposure has already proven not to translate into confidence — the missing piece is a mechanism that closes the loop between "used a tool once" and "feel capable"

**Opportunity 5 — Design for equitable reach, not institutional dependency**
**For** educators in under-resourced or government schools — the segment with the least institutional training access today
**Through** a product that works independent of school budget/infrastructure — low-bandwidth friendly, low-cost or free, ideally local-language capable
**Because** current access tracks institutional wealth rather than need; anything that depends on school-funded rollout will reproduce the same inequity

**Opportunity 6 — Design the entry point to build trust, not just deliver content**
**For** the meaningful skeptical/reluctant segment within the broad educator persona, not only eager early adopters
**Through** an onboarding that frames AI as amplifying the teacher's authority and easing workload — not replacing their expertise — starting with low-stakes, credibility-safe use cases
**Because** skepticism here is rooted in identity and workload threat, not lack of awareness; a purely instructional first experience will bounce this segment before it earns their trust

**Opportunity 7 — Include "constructive scepticism" as a taught skill**
**For** educators aiming not just to use AI themselves but to model responsible use for students (the "right behaviours" half of the original problem statement)
**Through** including how to evaluate, verify, and question AI output as one of the curriculum modules
**Because** it's a dimension no competitor in the landscape addresses, and it's a direct bridge between the MVP-measurable outcome (educator capability) and the longer-term strategic thesis (student behaviour)

## 6. Scope Down

### Chosen Direction

A structured, sequenced, AI-guided micro-learning experience that builds educators' foundational Tech & AI confidence through small, recurring practice loops. The curriculum covers a range of foundational skills, with evaluating AI output critically ("constructive scepticism") included as one of the core modules taught — not the defining theme of the product. Delivery is self-paced and AI-guided rather than dependent on scarce human trainers, and the first-session experience is explicitly designed to earn trust from a skeptical or reluctant educator, not just deliver content to an already-eager one.

This pulls together Opportunities 1, 2, 4, 6, and 7 from Section 5 as the core of the MVP, while treating 3 (owning the long-term "home base") as the direction this grows toward if the core loop works, and 5 (deep equity/localization) as a design principle to respect where possible but not a day-one build requirement.

### Why this
- It attacks the sharpest, most evidenced gap: 82% of educators get no formal guidance and existing tools add to platform fatigue rather than reducing it (Section 3.5/3.6) — sequencing is the single highest-leverage, most under-served need.
- It avoids the one constraint nothing else can route around: India's mandate needs 10M+ teachers trained against a fraction that many qualified trainers (Section 3.7). An AI-guided model scales without that bottleneck; a human-expert-led model inherits it.
- Evaluating AI output critically is included as a curriculum module rather than left out entirely, since it's a dimension none of the current competitors address (Section 3.4) — it strengthens the curriculum's relevance without needing to carry the whole product's positioning.
- A trust-first onboarding directly answers the identity/skepticism data (13–24% not eager, teachers viewing AI as a "cheating shortcut") rather than assuming the product only needs to serve people who are already sold.

### Why not the others
- *Full institutional/school-partnership distribution* (part of Opportunity 5) — selling into schools is slow (budget cycles, procurement, approvals); it can't produce 40–50 real users on a roughly one-week runway. Individual-educator acquisition is the only path fast enough to test.
- *Cohort-based peer learning* (part of Opportunity 2) — scheduling and coordinating live cohorts adds real build and ops overhead that isn't necessary to test the core hypothesis (does a sequenced, AI-guided loop build confidence better than the status quo?). Worth testing later, not in v1.
- *Deep localization / multilingual support* (Opportunity 5) — a real and evidenced equity gap, but not solvable well in days. Building it badly would be worse than deferring it honestly; the MVP will stay accessible-by-design (low-cost/free, mobile-friendly) without claiming to solve the language gap yet.
- *Building the full "ecosystem/home base" platform* (Opportunity 3) — too broad to build and validate quickly. The right sequencing is to prove the core learning loop retains people first; ecosystem breadth is what you earn after that, not before it.

### Evidence supporting this direction
- Section 3.5 data: 82% no formal guidance, 70% feel unprepared despite rising usage — supports sequencing as the core value prop over another tool.
- Section 3.7: India's trainer-supply shortfall relative to the 10M-teacher training mandate — supports an AI-guided, non-expert-bottlenecked delivery model.
- Section 3.6: 8 tools/educator average, 50% overwhelmed — supports one coherent guided experience over adding another point-solution.
- Section 4 insight on the skeptical/reluctant segment — supports a trust-earning onboarding design rather than an enthusiasm-assuming one.

### One explicit assumption worth flagging
The pilot's 40–50 real users will realistically come from whoever is reachable in the available time — likely urban, English-comfortable, network-reachable educators — even though the long-term persona is wider than that, including under-resourced government-school teachers who are harder to reach quickly. This isn't abandoning the broad persona; it's an honest scoping choice about who the *first* signal comes from. Worth deciding explicitly whether that's the right trade-off, or whether a specific reachable sub-segment should be named for the pilot.

| Solution | Who uses it | What's good | What's missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MagicSchool AI, Brisk, Edcafe, Diffit, SchoolAI** | Any teacher looking to save time on a specific task | Immediately useful, low-friction, builds passive tool familiarity | Teaches "click this button," not underlying understanding; contributes to platform fatigue rather than building confidence |
| **Khanmigo** | Teachers in schools using Khan Academy | Reputable, free for teachers | Built around student tutoring first; teacher capability-building is a side feature |
| **General-purpose AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot directly)** | Educators experimenting on their own | Free/cheap entry point, direct hands-on learning-by-doing | No structure, no evaluation of whether the educator is using it well or safely; easy to plateau at surface-level use |

### Manual Solutions
*(Structured, but delivered by a person or institution rather than a scalable product.)*

| Solution | Who uses it | What's good | What's missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One-off school/district workshops** | Teachers at schools with PD budget | Live, can be tailored to the room, human support in the moment | Single session, no reinforcement, quality varies hugely by facilitator — one district leader described their own first AI PD as "overwhelming" and something that "scared" them |
| **In-house programs built by individual school leaders** | Schools where a motivated administrator builds their own training after external options fell short | Can be genuinely well-tailored to that specific school's context | Not scalable — depends entirely on one motivated individual existing at that school; most schools don't have this |
| **B.Ed / pre-service teacher training** | New teachers entering the workforce | Would be the ideal upstream fix if it existed properly | AI literacy isn't a required part of most curricula — this channel is largely absent today |

### Workarounds
*(Informal, unmeasured, but where most educators actually are today.)*

| Solution | Who uses it | What's good | What's missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **YouTube tutorials** | Educators searching ad hoc for "how to use X tool" | Free, abundant, on-demand | No sequencing, no quality control, no way to know what to search for next |
| **WhatsApp / Facebook teacher groups** | Educators seeking peer recommendations | Trusted (peer-sourced), contextually relevant, free | Unstructured, advice quality varies, no accountability or progression tracking |
| **Colleague recommendations / trial-and-error** | Nearly every educator at some point | Zero cost, socially low-risk | Entirely dependent on who happens to be nearby; doesn't scale and leaves gaps invisible |
| **Learning informally from students themselves** | Educators in classrooms where students are more AI-fluent | Immediate, real-world relevant | Can undermine teacher authority/credibility rather than build it; not a repeatable or reliable channel |

### Insights

Structured help exists only at the extremes — a national mandate (broad, shallow, one-directional) and paid general courses (rigorous, generic, inaccessible to many). Nearly everything in between — the mid-tier, role-relevant, ongoing support layer — is filled by informal workarounds that are free but entirely unstructured and invisible to measurement. This is the clearest confirmation yet of the wedge from Section 6: a sequenced, AI-guided, trust-earning experience isn't just a nice-to-have differentiator, it's occupying a genuinely empty middle of this landscape.
